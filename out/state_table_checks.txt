We estimate what broad-based categorical eligibility (BBCE) would add to the 2024 SNAP index in Brookings' State Safety Net Interactive.
We rebuilt the interactive's sample from the 2015-2019 CPS ASEC public-use files, following its July 2026 data appendix (v3).
We formed one family around each CPS family reference person, with any spouse or cohabiting partner, and merged two reference persons who cohabit into one family.
We included children under 19, children 19-23 in school full time and disabled children under 24, as long as a biological parent lived in the family.
We disregarded other household members.
We kept families with a child under 18 in households with nobody 65 or older and no foreign-born members, and weighted each by the reference person's ASEC weight.
We counted a member as disabled who received SSI in their own right.
We also counted members 15 or older who said a disability kept them out of the labor force, or that illness or disability kept them from working the prior year.
We also counted members 15-18 who reported difficulty on any of the CPS's six disability questions, and children under 18 who received SSI.
When a parent reported receiving SSI on behalf of a child, we counted the family's youngest child under 18 as disabled.
For the reference person and any spouse or partner, we summed prior-year wages, self-employment and farm income and floored each person's sum at zero.
We brought those earnings to 2024 dollars with annual average CPI-U and entered them as wages.
We then raised them 7%, where our federal EITC best fits Brookings' published values for the family types with no disabled member.
At that scale, our federal-rules SNAP index for Wisconsin comes within 5% of Brookings' for each family type with no disabled member.
For each Wisconsin family type with a disabled member, ours trails Brookings' by 15-19%, and our federal SSI alone exceeds Brookings' federal and state SSI by 10-56%.
We entered no children's earnings, other income, assets, housing costs or child care costs.
We placed each family in every state and computed its July 2024 SNAP with PolicyEngine-US 2.100.0 under federal rules and under the state's BBCE rules.
We gave no family a utility allowance and kept full-time students in the SNAP unit.
We also set TANF to zero, so that it counted neither as SNAP income nor as a route to categorical eligibility.
Under PolicyEngine-US's own TANF rules, we estimate that 0.8% of the families that gain from BBCE in the 35 jurisdictions receive TANF, which would make them categorically eligible without BBCE.
If we leave out their gains, the median gain across the 35 jurisdictions falls from $91.44 to $90.57, and the gains in California and Wisconsin fall by about 6%.
Across the 48 states other than Alaska and Hawaii, plus DC, Brookings' SNAP index falls 27 cents for each dollar of its TANF index.
Because we set TANF to zero, our federal-rules index falls 1 cent for each dollar of Brookings' TANF index.
PolicyEngine-US treated every member we counted as disabled as meeting SSI's disability criteria.
It then applied SSI's 2024 limit on substantial gainful activity, so members earning over $1,550 a month received no SSI.
It computed federal SSI and the state supplements it models, and counted both as SNAP income.
As federal rules require, PolicyEngine-US exempted families with an SSI recipient or a member 60 or older from SNAP's gross income test.
We left the state minimum benefits in DC, Maryland and New Jersey, and DC's local supplement, out of both scenarios.
We multiplied each July benefit by 12 and averaged it over every family, including families with no SNAP.
We repeat the estimate without the 7% raise in the column bbce_adds_usd_at_cpi_u_earnings.
We converted Brookings' 2024 index (file 1_noimmig v7) from thousands of dollars to dollars.
The gross income limits in the second column match FNS's August 2024 BBCE chart.
We took the net income tests, and the rules for households with an elderly or disabled member, from PolicyEngine-US parameters.
